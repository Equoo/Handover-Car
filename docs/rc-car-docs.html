Driving a car through the internet

A remote-control car you pilot from anywhere over the 5G mobile network, while watching its camera feed with almost no delay. This page explains the whole thing from zero — no prior knowledge assumed.

01 — The idea in one breath

It's a video call, except one caller is a car

Imagine a video call. Normally both people hold a phone. Here, one "person" is a small RC car. The car sends you live video from a camera bolted to its front. You send back not words, but steering and throttle. You see through its eyes and drive it, and the car could be on the other side of the city.

Everything in this project exists to make that one loop work: your controls go out, the car's video comes back, and both happen fast enough that driving actually feels responsive.

The mental model

You (a laptop) ⇄ a rented computer on the internet (the "middleman") ⇄ the car (a Raspberry Pi with a camera, a modem, and motors). The middleman exists purely so the two ends can find each other. More on why below.

02 — Why it isn't trivial

Two problems make this genuinely hard

Problem 1 — The car has no "address"

On a home internet connection your router has a public address other computers can reach. But on a mobile network (4G/5G), your phone — and this car's modem — does not. The carrier hides thousands of customers behind shared addresses (this is called CGNAT). So you literally cannot dial the car directly. It can reach out, but nothing can reach in.

Analogy

The car is like someone at a huge company with no direct phone line. You can't call their desk. The fix: both of you call a switchboard that everyone can reach, and it connects the two calls. That switchboard is the rented server on the internet (a VPS at address 37.59.106.4).

Problem 2 — Delay ruins driving

If the video lags by even half a second, you crash before you see the wall. So the whole system is obsessed with low latency. Two consequences shape every design choice:

• It uses UDP, never TCP. TCP guarantees every packet arrives in order — but to do that it stops and waits for lost packets, which adds delay. For live video and controls, a slightly glitchy-but-instant feed beats a perfect-but-late one. UDP just fires packets and doesn't wait.

• Video is compressed by a dedicated chip. Raw camera video is enormous. The Raspberry Pi 4 has a special hardware chip that squeezes it into the H.264 format (the same one used by YouTube/Blu-ray) without bogging down its main processor.

04 — Journey A

What happens when you turn the wheel

Follow a single steering command from your fingers all the way to the wheels. This is a genuine sequence, so it's numbered:

Command path · you → car
Client (your PC)

You press a key or move a stick. The client packages it as a "MasterInput" message — a steering number and a throttle number — and sends it to the server. Sent as fast, no-guarantee UDP, because a fresh command is always coming right behind it.

Server (switchboard)

The server looks up which car this driver is subscribed to, then forwards the command to that car's current network address.

Slave (the Pi)

The car's brain receives the command and writes it as a simple line of text — e.g. 1500 1486\n — down a serial cable to the Arduino.

Arduino

The Arduino reads that line and converts the two numbers into PWM pulses — the pulse "language" that hobby servos and motor controllers understand.

Servo + motor

The steering servo turns; the motor speeds up or slows down. The car moves. Total round-trip is a fraction of a second.

Why it's split this way

The Raspberry Pi is great at internet and video but bad at precise real-time electrical timing. The Arduino is the opposite. So the Pi handles the network and hands off the twitchy motor-timing work to the Arduino over a plain serial cable.

05 — Journey B

What happens so you can see the road

Now the other direction: a single camera frame's trip from the car's lens to your screen.

Video path · car → you
Camera + streamer

The camera captures a frame. The streamer feeds it straight into the Pi's hardware H.264 chip, which crushes it from megabytes down to a few kilobytes.

Streamer (packaging)

The compressed frame is chopped into small network packets (RTP, the standard for live media) and given a little error-correction padding (FEC) so the receiver can rebuild a few lost packets without asking for resends.

Server (switchboard)

Packets hit the server, which blindly forwards them to whichever driver most recently "subscribed" to this car's video.

Client (jitter buffer)

Your PC collects packets in a tiny 40-millisecond buffer to smooth out the internet's uneven timing, puts them back in order, and uses FEC to patch small losses.

Client (decode + display)

A hardware video decoder (NVIDIA / Intel / AMD if available, otherwise software) turns H.264 back into pictures, and GStreamer paints them into the window. You see the road.

The stats overlay

While this streams, the client corner-overlays live telemetry: frames per second, decode delay, your ping, the car's ping, packet loss %, and current video bitrate — so you can see the link's health at a glance, like a cockpit gauge cluster.

06 — The clever tricks

Three ideas that make it actually work

1. The "hole-punch" so the car can be reached

Remember the car has no reachable address. The trick: the driver sends one tiny packet to the server first. That packet teaches the server "here's where this driver lives right now." From then on, the server forwards the car's video to that remembered address. Both ends reach out to the middleman, so neither needs to be reachable directly.

2. Adaptive quality (ABR) — cruise control for video

Mobile signal constantly changes. So the client watches its packet-loss meter and, every few seconds, decides a new target quality using a simple rule: lose too many packets → cut the bitrate hard; link is clean → nudge it back up. That target is relayed through the server to the streamer, which retunes the hardware encoder on the fly. The result: the picture gets softer instead of freezing when signal drops, and sharpens again when it recovers. It's a full feedback loop across three programs.

Analogy

Like a driver easing off the gas on a bad road and speeding up on smooth tarmac — automatically, several times a second.

3. Talking to the video chip directly

Most projects would just run the Pi's ready-made camera tool. This project's streamer instead talks straight to the encoder chip (the Linux device /dev/video11), which shaves off delay and unlocks live bitrate changes. It's the most technically ambitious piece of the codebase.

07 — The safety net

What stops a runaway car when the signal drops

A cellular link will drop sometimes — that's a certainty, not an if. Without protection, a dropped connection means "last command was full throttle" and the car vanishes into a wall. Two safeguards prevent this:

• Dead-man's switch (Arduino). The Arduino expects a fresh command constantly. If 100 milliseconds pass with no valid command, it automatically centres the steering and cuts the throttle to neutral. No signal, no movement.

• Relentless reconnection (Pi). The car's software keeps retrying its connection to the server forever, so a blip becomes a brief pause rather than a dead car.

Design note

The safety ultimately rests on the Arduino's timeout. The higher-level software doesn't independently force a stop — it relies on the commands simply going quiet, which trips the dead-man's switch. Simple and robust, but worth knowing where the guarantee actually lives.

09 — Honest state of the project

Known issues & rough edges

This is a working prototype under active development, not a finished product. If you're explaining it to someone, it's fair to be upfront about what still needs attention:

Security

A private SSH key is committed to the repo

The file os/root/.ssh/id_rsa is a real private key checked into version control (the project's own TODO says "do not push ssh keys"). It should be rotated and scrubbed from history — treat it as compromised.

Bug

Car IDs can collide with multiple cars

The server picks the first free slot for each car but never marks it as taken, so a second car would grab the same video port. Fine with one car; breaks with several.

Inconsistency

Video profile comment doesn't match the code

The streamer's comment says "baseline profile" (the low-latency choice from the plan) but the code actually sets "high profile." One of the two should be corrected.

Cleanup

Dead code & stale docs

An unused old.rs, large commented-out blocks, and modem setup code that mixes up two connection modes (NCM vs RNDIS). CLAUDE.md also still describes an older WebRTC plan that the code has since moved past.

10 — Glossary

Jargon decoder

Every unavoidable technical term, in one line each:

CGNAT
Why a phone/5G modem has no reachable address — the carrier hides many customers behind shared addresses.
VPS
A small computer you rent on the internet. Here it runs the relay/switchboard.
UDP
A "fire and forget" way of sending data. Fast, but doesn't guarantee delivery — perfect for live video and controls.
TCP
The careful opposite of UDP: guarantees delivery and order, but waits for lost data — too slow for real-time driving.
H.264
A hugely popular video compression format. The Pi 4 has a chip that does it in hardware.
RTP
The standard way of chopping live audio/video into network packets.
FEC
Forward Error Correction — extra padding sent with the video so the receiver can rebuild a few lost packets without asking for resends.
Jitter buffer
A tiny holding area (40 ms here) that smooths out the internet's uneven packet timing before playback.
Bitrate
How much data per second the video uses. Higher = sharper but heavier; the ABR loop tunes it live.
ABR
Adaptive Bitrate — automatically raising/lowering video quality to match the current network.
Servo / PWM
A servo is a small precise motor (steering); PWM is the pulse signal that tells it where to point.
ESC
Electronic Speed Controller — the part that drives the car's main motor from a PWM signal.
Serial / UART
A simple two-wire cable for sending text between the Pi and the Arduino.
libcamera / V4L2
The Linux systems for talking to cameras and video hardware on the Pi.
GStreamer
A toolkit for building video pipelines — used on the client to receive, decode, and display.
AT commands
The old text "language" used to configure cellular modems (e.g. to check signal strength).
RSRP / RSRQ / SINR
Cellular signal-quality numbers — the modem dashboard colours them green/yellow/red.
